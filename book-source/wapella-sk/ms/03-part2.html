Part Two

The People Who Came

Chapter 3

Crofters and Pioneers

Within a year of the railway’s arrival, the first organized group of settlers reached the district. They came from about as far away, in every sense, as it was possible to come: the windswept islands of the Outer Hebrides.

From the Western Isles

In the Hebrides of the early 1880s, crofting families farmed tiny plots of rented land, owed rent they could rarely pay, and lived under landlords who wanted fewer tenants, not more. Lady Emily Gordon Cathcart owned large estates there, including the islands of Benbecula and South Uist. In 1882 she proposed sending a group of families from her estates to the Canadian North-West to found a Gaelic-speaking settlement. Her plan has been judged harshly by historians in Scotland, where she is remembered as a landlord who pushed her tenants to emigrate. For the families who went, it was a hard road and a new start at the same time.

Each family received £100 sterling, about $500, to cover passage, tools and supplies. It was a loan, not a gift: families were to pay it back with five percent interest once they were established. The first party, about fourteen families and several single men, crossed the Atlantic in the spring of 1883 under almost constant iceberg warnings. The crossing took about fourteen days. They then rode colonist cars through northern Ontario and reached Moosomin around the end of May. They were led by Donald McDiarmid, who had been sent out the year before to scout land.

The names of the heads of household read like a roll call from the islands: Donald McDonald, Roderick McDonald, Angus McCormick, Lachlan McPherson, John McDonald, William McPherson, Alex McPherson, Alex McDonald, and Ewen McKinnon, who came with his sister Mary. Single men in the party included Donald McKay, Archie McDonald and John Buchanan. About nine of the families were Protestant and five Roman Catholic, and by every account they lived side by side without trouble.

A second, larger party followed in 1884. They landed at Quebec, took the train to Owen Sound, and crossed the Great Lakes on the steamer Algoma, which was making its first voyage.

Tents, dugouts and fire

Nothing about the first years was easy. The families lived in tents while implements and supplies were gathered, then dug temporary homes into hillsides. A prairie fire had swept the valley of Pipestone Creek, and the settlers crossed the burned ground at night. Hail destroyed crops in 1885. A Moosomin newspaper reported in February 1886 that one family had lost their home to fire and suffered terrible frostbite. Some families could not repay their debts and moved on to other land.

But the colony survived and, in time, prospered. The crofters founded three settlements within about fifteen miles of Moosomin, named St. Andrew’s, Benbecula and Iona, and their district became one of the most productive in the region. Gaelic was still spoken by their children. By the third generation most of it had gone, but the names remained.

The Scottish settlement south of Wapella

South of the Wapella townsite, a Scottish settlement grew up from 1883, associated with St. Andrew’s and St. Paul’s. Former crofters took up land across a wide district south of town over the next few years. St. Paul’s Cemetery, about nine kilometres south of Wapella, recorded its first burial in 1883, the same year the first crofters arrived. (One stone there, for Thomas Armstrong, gives a date of death in July 1882, a year earlier still. The records don’t explain the difference.) The family names carved in its stones tell where its people came from: Ferguson, Armstrong, MacDonald, Campbell, MacLean, MacKenzie, McFadyen, McPherson, McRury, McQueen.

Among those buried at St. Paul’s is a Donald MacDiarmid, born on Christmas Day 1849 and died in January 1937. Whether he is the same Donald McDiarmid who led the crofters to Moosomin in 1883 is a question for a family historian to answer; the spelling of Highland names wandered a great deal in those years.

St. Andrew’s Roman Catholic Church, its hall and its cemetery, about eleven kilometres south of Wapella, are today a designated Municipal Heritage Property of the Rural Municipality of Martin, a lasting mark of the Catholic families among the island settlers.

English neighbours

English settlers arrived in the district in 1884. Farther south, in the Earlswood district near Red Jacket, a small Presbyterian cemetery and chapel served the families there; its earliest recorded burials date from the late 1880s. The Earlswood Cemetery and Chapel is also a designated Municipal Heritage Property of the RM of Martin.

Together, the Scots, the English and the railway town formed one side of Wapella’s early community. The other side was taking shape north of the tracks.

Chapter 4

The Colony

Northeast of Wapella, in the poplar bluffs, a group of Jewish immigrants who had never been allowed to own land in their old country set out to become farmers. Against long odds, they succeeded. Their settlement is remembered as the first successful Jewish farm colony in Canada.

Why they came

In the Russian Empire of the 1880s, Jews faced laws that restricted where they could live and travel, barred them from owning land, limited their schooling and forced long years of military service on their sons. After the assassination of Czar Alexander II in 1881, waves of pogroms swept through Jewish communities, killing many and leaving thousands homeless. Families from Bessarabia and southern Russia began to leave. Some went to Palestine. Many more went west, to the United States, to England, and to Canada.

Canada’s West had just been opened. Manitoba had joined Confederation in 1870 and British Columbia in 1871, and the railway across the prairie was completed by 1885. Land was available for anyone willing to work it, and a few people in London and Montreal began to ask whether Jewish refugees might find a future on it.

Heppner and Landau

The first was John Heppner. In 1886, Hermann Landau, an Anglo-Jewish financier in London who also acted for the CPR, sent Heppner and four young Jewish men to Canada with $2,000 for implements, cattle, tools, provisions and seed. Landau hoped to attract settlers to the railway’s lands and, at the same time, give Russian Jews a refuge. The Heppner family settled a few miles northeast of Wapella. Some sources place their arrival in 1887 rather than 1886, and one later encyclopedia gives 1894 as the year the colony was formally organized. The settlement itself, everyone agrees, began in the late 1880s.

Abraham Klenman

The man who turned a few homesteads into a colony was Abraham Klenman. Born around 1830 near Soroca, in Bessarabia, he had spent his life on the land: he rented farmland as a young man and later managed an estate for an absentee Russian landlord. He believed Jews could only renew themselves by returning to farming. In 1888, in his late fifties, he arrived in Montreal with a large family: his second wife Pearl, their children, two daughters from his first marriage, and his son-in-law Solomon Barish with the Barishes’ three children.

In the fall of 1888 Klenman and Jacob Silver were chosen to find land in the West. In Winnipeg they met Lauchlan Alexander Hamilton, the railway’s land commissioner. After rejecting land near Deloraine and near Regina, they chose the wooded country beside the Heppners. Klenman arranged something unusual: the Department of the Interior and the CPR agreed to swap land so that the Jewish families’ homesteads could sit next to each other. That mattered. Orthodox Jewish prayer requires a minyan, a gathering of at least ten men, and on the Sabbath people could not travel far to meet. A scattered colony could not have held together.

Winter caught Klenman before his house was built. He spent his first prairie winter in a dugout cellar roofed with poles and straw. In the spring of 1889 he finished a log house with a thatched straw roof, in the style of the Russian peasant houses he knew, and his family and several other immigrants joined him. He paid the ten-dollar homestead registration fee for many of the newcomers out of his own pocket.

The families

Between the spring of 1889 and the fall of 1892, twenty-eight Jewish families followed Klenman to the Wapella district. By 1907 about fifty families had come. Most were from southern Russia and Bessarabia, with some from Romania, Galicia and Lithuania. A few had farmed in North Dakota, and others had worked building the railway, but most had been tailors, shoemakers, labourers and pedlars. They arrived with little or no money, at a time when wheat sold for about twenty-five cents a bushel.

The names of the colony’s families have come down through the memories of their children: Klenman, Barish, Heppner, Brotman, Pelenovsky, Kaplun, Isman, Jacobson, Kliman, Leonoff, Wasserman, Edess, Goldenberg, and, for a short time, Bronfman.

Solomon Barish stayed behind in Montreal for a while, working in a cigar factory. When a doctor advised country air, he trained in Chicago as a shochet, a ritual slaughterer, so the colony could have kosher meat. The first settlers had lived largely on prairie chickens they trapped, and these too had to be slaughtered according to Jewish law. Barish reached Winnipeg in 1892 with $100, just enough to get to the farm and buy oxen, a plough and a wagon.

The farmer-rabbi

In 1889 Edel Brotman, who had been a rabbi in Galicia and had reached Montreal in 1886, took up a homestead at Wapella with three of his sons. For about sixteen years, until 1906, he was the colony’s farmer-rabbi: he worked his land through the week and led the community’s religious life. There was no synagogue. Services were held in settlers’ homes, and by the early years there were three separate prayer groups. Families who lived far away rode in by horse before the holidays and stayed for two days. The congregation was known as the Wapella Hebrew Congregation.

Weddings followed the old customs, prairie or not. On December 25, 1898, Fanny Pelenovsky married Sam Brotman outdoors, under the sky, at roughly fifty degrees below zero, with Rabbi Brotman officiating. Another marriage contract, the ketubah of Alter Kaplun and the daughter of Kalman Isman, was written out by hand on the farm.

Abraham Klenman donated land for the colony’s cemetery. In his last years, when he could no longer do a full day’s farm work, he taught Hebrew to the colony’s boys as they prepared for their Bar Mitzvah, and he subscribed to a Yiddish newspaper from New York to follow the news of the wider world. He died near Wapella in 1910 and was buried in the Jewish cemetery at Brandon.

Life on a colony farm

Houses. The first homes were log buildings plastered with mud, with sod or straw roofs. Wood stoves heated them, and fires were common. The Barish house burned in January 1917; a Klenman house caught fire during the baking of matzo for Passover.

Furniture. Most of it was homemade. Early on, families slept on hay spread on the floor.

Water. Families melted snow, hauled water and dug wells by hand. The Barish well went down sixty feet before it struck a spring in gravel.

Food. Poultry was slaughtered according to Jewish law, and kosher beef was ordered from Brandon or Winnipeg. Families picked wild berries, grew vegetables, and put up cottage cheese and butter for the winter. Kosher kitchens kept separate sets of dishes.

Money. Cash was scarce. Settlers cut poplar and hauled it by ox team to Moosomin, about twenty-five miles away, or to Wapella, selling it for a dollar to a dollar and a quarter a load. Children dug seneca root, which sold for medicine.

Clothes. Few, and handed down. Shoes were precious.

Oxen, wheat and learning

The settlers farmed with oxen because they could not afford horses; horses came later, and a steam tractor in the 1920s. Wheat was the main cash crop, first Red Fife, then Marquis, which suffered badly from rust, then newer rust-resistant varieties. Families also kept cattle, horses and sheep.

The second generation went to school and, in some cases, to college. Harry Barish studied agriculture and won a prize for his Red Fife wheat in 1908. Sam Barish attended the young University of Saskatchewan and brought scientific methods home: crop rotation with hay, and a purebred Angus herd that grew to about 125 head. In 1915 the Barishes bought a McLaughlin automobile.

In 1910 Harry Klenman, Abraham’s grandson, bought the first steam threshing outfit among the Jewish farmers. He ran it for thirty years, until 1940. Klenman later served overseas in both World Wars. After his death in 1955 his steam engine went to the museum of old tractors at Rocanville, where it is preserved today.

School

The colony’s first school opened in 1898. The settlers had asked the government for help with a building and in the end raised the money themselves. Ben Barish was thirteen when it was built. The teacher, Ada Blanche Jenner, came from Nova Scotia and earned $480 a year. Jewish children were the only Jewish pupils in a district school, and over time they were simply part of it. Country schools stood about every six miles, so children could walk to them, though many also herded livestock before and after class. The one-room schools closed in the 1950s, and children were bused into town.

Free time meant visiting, dances and barn dances, skating, skiing, sleigh rides, piano lessons, and plays, including a play for the festival of Purim.

The frost of 1901

For its first fifteen years the colony received no help from the government or from Jewish charities. That changed in 1901, when a killing frost destroyed the district’s crops. Twenty-seven Jewish farmers borrowed from the Jewish Colonization Association, the fund established by Baron Maurice de Hirsch, through its New York agency. It was the colony’s first outside aid. They repaid every dollar within seventeen years.

Sunday and Saturday

The Jewish farmers rested on Saturday, their Sabbath, and worked their fields on Sunday. At threshing time they hired others to work for them on Saturdays. For years nobody objected. In 1921, however, some of them were summoned under the Lord’s Day Act for working on a Sunday. A Christian neighbour, Bert Talmay, spoke up for them in court, and the case was dismissed. Bert Talmay is buried in the Wapella Community Cemetery; the transcription gives his years as 1873 to 1953.

The long goodbye

The colony began with about forty families farming roughly ten thousand acres. About forty years later, a study by Louis Rosenberg counted thirteen Jewish farmers working 3,500 acres. Homestead land had run out and land prices had risen, so new immigrants could no longer start here. Children were sent to the cities for high school and university and mostly stayed there. Young men had trouble finding Jewish wives on the prairie. Machinery reduced the need for children’s labour on the farm. The historian Cyril Leonoff noted that the Jewish settlement shrank at about the same rate as its Scottish and English neighbours, part of a pattern repeated across North America.

By 1962, seventy-five years after Heppner’s arrival, little of the colony remained. Eli, Ben and Sam Barish sold their 1,100 acres between 1958 and 1962 when they retired. Barney Kaplun, son of Alter Kaplun, was the last Jewish farmer in the district in 1969, farming about a thousand acres. Kaplun’s family still worked a section and a half in the area at the time.

In the summer of 1969 three retired Barish brothers, Sam at 72, Ben at 81 and Eli at 88, made one last trip home together, driving about three thousand miles from Vancouver with Cyril Leonoff, whose grandfather and great-grandfather had homesteaded beside them in 1889. Leonoff’s account of that journey, published by the Manitoba Historical Society, remains the best source on the colony. Asked about his life, Sam Barish called farming the most independent life there is.

The Wapella colony was the forerunner of about a dozen Jewish farm settlements on the prairies, including Hirsch, Sonnenfeld and Lipton in Saskatchewan. It trained immigrants and young farmhands, and it proved something many had doubted. The Wapella Jewish Cemetery, somewhere in the old colony lands north of town, is its quiet monument.

Chapter 5

The Bronfmans

Of all the families who passed through the Wapella colony, one would become famous around the world. Their stay was short, and their first prairie years were hard.

In 1889, Yechiel Bronfman, also known as Ekiel, and his wife Mindel came to Canada with their children, fleeing the pogroms of the Russian Empire. Yechiel had worked with tobacco in Bessarabia. The family settled on a homestead near Wapella as part of the Jewish colony. Their son Samuel was born that same year, 1889, in the Soroki region of Bessarabia, so he came to the prairie as a baby.

The family tried to grow tobacco, the crop Yechiel knew. It failed in the prairie cold. They turned to whatever work there was: labour on the railway, a sawmill, cutting and selling firewood, fishing, and trading horses. Around 1892 they left Wapella for Brandon, Manitoba, where Yechiel started a firewood delivery business with his three eldest sons.

From there the family moved into the hotel business. In 1903 they ran the Anglo-American Hotel in Emerson, Manitoba, and young Sam, still a boy, noticed that the real profit was in the bar. That observation became an empire. Sam Bronfman founded Distillers Corporation in 1924, acquired the Seagram company in 1928, and built it into the largest spirits business in the world. He became one of the most influential Canadian Jews of the twentieth century, and his family became famous for its philanthropy as well as its fortune.

The Encyclopedia of Saskatchewan puts it simply: Canada’s famous Bronfman family got its start on a homestead in the Wapella area in 1889. It is a reminder that the poplar bluffs north of town once held families whose children would go on to remarkable lives, and that every one of them started with a dugout, a log house, and a winter to get through.
