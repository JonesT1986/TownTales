Part Three

Building a Town

Chapter 6

Main Street Rises

At the turn of the twentieth century the prairie West filled with settlers faster than anyone had predicted. Wapella, with a railway station, good farmland and an early start, was ready to grow.

A newspaperman arrives

In 1899 a printer named Edward S. Zinng came west and settled at Wapella. Born in New Hamburg, Ontario, in 1872 and trained in the printing trade at Hanover, he founded the Wapella Post, the town’s weekly newspaper, and edited it for decades. A town with its own newspaper was a town that meant to last. The paper carried the news of crops and prices, council meetings and church suppers, births, weddings and funerals, the scores of ball games and the arrivals and departures at the station.

Zinng was more than an editor. He sat on Wapella’s first town council, served as president of the local Board of Trade, was a Past Master of the local Masonic Lodge, and in 1907 was president of the Western Canadian Press Association. Men like him, often the same handful of names on every committee, built the institutions of small prairie towns.

The hotels

Every railway town needed hotels, and Wapella had several over the years. The Queen’s Hotel was already standing around 1890. In 1901 a new hotel went up, built originally as the Mundell Hotel and later known as the Commercial Hotel. It is counted among the oldest commercial buildings in town and is listed on the Canadian Register of Historic Places.

A Mundell name also appears in the Wapella Community Cemetery: Bertha Higgins Mundell, who died in 1923. Whether she was connected to the hotel’s builder is one of the many questions a family history could answer.

Incorporation, 1903

In 1903 Wapella was incorporated as a town. It was a statement of confidence. Saskatchewan itself would not become a province for another two years; the town was still in the North-West Territories when it took on the responsibilities of a council, a budget and bylaws. That same year the CPR built its branch line through the district, and the next few years were the busiest in Wapella’s history.

By 1911 the population was approaching five hundred. The federal census that year counted 447 people in the town.

Chapter 7

The Town Hall

If one building tells the story of Wapella’s ambitions, it is the Old Town Hall: two storeys of buff brick, a grand double staircase, and an opera hall that few towns in Saskatchewan could match.

Two years after the town incorporated, its council planned a town hall worthy of the place. The design came in 1906 from the Regina architectural firm of Hutchinson and Nobles, who drew it in the formal Classical Revival style. Construction was finished late in 1907.

Like many small-town halls of its time, it was built to do everything. Inside were the council chambers and the town office, the jail, the fire hall and an opera hall, or auditorium. It was, by the account of its heritage designation, the most prominent building in the community and a sophisticated example of an early Saskatchewan civic building.

The opera hall

The upstairs auditorium was the heart of Wapella’s social life for decades. It hosted theatrical performances, dances, lectures and debates. At the time it was built, only a few town hall auditoriums in Saskatchewan could equal it. For families on isolated farms, a night at the town hall, a travelling show, a debate or a dance, was the event of the season.

The building served as Wapella’s town hall from 1907 until 2003, almost a hundred years. It was designated a Municipal Heritage Property by the Town of Wapella on September 15, 2008, and listed on the Canadian Register of Historic Places in 2010. Its heritage statement describes its solid, substantial character as a reminder of the established, optimistic and prosperous town that Wapella was when it was built.

The school

The town’s school was another point of pride. A postcard from the early twentieth century shows a solid stone school building, a far cry from the log and frame schoolhouses of the 1880s, and a sign that Wapella expected its children to stay and its town to grow.

Phones and the rural municipality

On January 13, 1913, the Rural Municipality of Martin No. 122 was incorporated, taking over local government for the farm country around the town. The first telephones reached the old colony district at about the same time. The RM still keeps a year-by-year history drawn from its own minute books.

Chapter 8

Hard Seasons

Prairie towns were built in good years and tested in bad ones. Wapella had its share of both, and not every chapter of its history is a comfortable one to read.

Frost, rust and drought

The killing frost of 1901 was the first great test, and the district’s farmers came through it on borrowed money they paid back in full. Rust attacked the Marquis wheat. Hail came without warning. But nothing compared with the 1930s.

The Depression brought roughly ten dry years in a row to the district. Yields that normally ran twenty to twenty-five bushels an acre fell to between two and five. Grasshoppers took much of what the drought left, and 1937 was a rust year that ended in crop failure. It was the hardest stretch the district’s farmers had faced since the frost of 1901. In 1937 the Barish family sold most of their livestock but kept their purebred breeding stock, sending forty head to a farm at Rivers, Manitoba, so they could rebuild the herd when the rain came back.

The Klan in Wapella

In 1927 and 1928, the Ku Klux Klan swept through Saskatchewan on an organizing drive that preached against Catholics, French Canadians and immigrants. Chapters were formed in more than a hundred Saskatchewan towns. Wapella was one of them. Klan Number 94 started with fourteen members, according to the Klan’s own files, now held at the Saskatchewan Archives. The detail was uncovered by Kevin Weedmark, editor of the Moosomin World-Spectator, in his investigation of the Klan in Saskatchewan. The movement collapsed in the early 1930s.

It belongs in this book because it happened, and because it sits beside the other things that happened here: a Christian neighbour standing up in court for Jewish farmers in 1921, Catholic and Protestant crofters living side by side, and a town hall where people of every background danced on Saturday nights. A town’s history includes its failures as well as its kindnesses.

The war years

The Wapella Post was still publishing during the Second World War. About 145 issues from the war years, apparently from 1943 and 1944, have been digitized and can be searched through the Saskatchewan Historic Newspapers collection of the Provincial Archives of Saskatchewan. They are the best window we have into Wapella’s wartime life, and a project for any reader who wants to find more stories for the next edition of this book.

Wapella’s men served overseas in both World Wars. Harry Klenman, who ran the colony’s steam threshing outfit, was among them.

Chapter 9

Diamonds and Ice

For a town that never had more than a few hundred people, Wapella has produced a remarkable sporting record: one of the finest summers in the history of prairie baseball, and a string of hockey championships across seventy years.

The summer of 1924

On April 3, 1924, Wapella held a community meeting about baseball. The residents decided that if the citizens could guarantee the money, the town should field a professional team. They did, and the result was a season that newspapers across the West were still talking about by fall.

The team opened its season at a tournament in Grenfell on May 24 and won its first twenty-three games in a row. The first loss came at Wolseley on July 2, after a punishing stretch of travel and back-to-back games. Then they kept winning. They took a tournament at Carlyle, beating Wawota in the final. At Oxbow, in a seven-team event with a $500 prize, the final against Sherwood was still scoreless after seven innings; Wapella declined to keep playing, and Sherwood was awarded the game. More wins followed at Arcola and Melita.

In late July came the biggest test: the Brandon Grays. On July 24 Wapella hosted a doubleheader and won both games, with Allan “Lefty” Armstrong throwing a one-hitter in the second. Two days later the teams met again in Brandon. The Grays won the afternoon game, but that evening Armstrong threw another one-hitter and Wapella won 8–0. They took the four-game series three games to one.

When the season ended, the Regina Leader-Post totted up the record: fifty-six games, fifty-one wins and five losses, 131 runs scored against twenty allowed. Wapella had entered twenty tournaments, taken first money in sixteen and second in one, and shared third in the rest, winning about $2,600 in prize money. The paper called them the most successful ball club in Saskatchewan that season. The town’s population was about four hundred.

The 1924 Wapella team

Armstrong was later inducted into the Regina Sports Hall of Fame as a member of the Regina Nationals. John “Whit” Meredith, who had pitched for the Brandon Grays, was inducted into the Manitoba Baseball Hall of Fame in 1998.

The season was reconstructed from 1924 newspaper reports by Joe McFarland for its hundredth anniversary in 2024.

Where most of the players came from, and where they went after that summer, nobody has yet been able to find out. Several of the names, Embury, Clements and Morrison among them, also appear in the Wapella cemetery records, which suggests that at least some of the team were local men. If your family has a photograph or a story from the 1924 season, it belongs in the next edition.

The Black Hawks

In winter, Wapella played hockey. The senior Wapella Black Hawks, who played from the mid-1940s into the 1950s, had a lineup that at one point included five Holloway brothers, the sons of Alexander and Dorothy Holloway. Alexander Holloway (1889–1955) and Dorothy Holloway (1901–1998) are both buried in the Wapella Community Cemetery. The Black Hawks won the South Saskatchewan Intermediate B title in 1953, and in 1954 they were provincial Senior B champions, a title that still stands in Hockey Saskatchewan’s list of past champions.

Their goaltender, Don Purdy, started with the team in the 1944 season and played five seasons. In December 2024, at the age of one hundred, he was still telling stories of those years to a sports reporter.

The Black Hawks name came back again and again. A later senior team called the Wapella Black Hawks won the provincial Senior D championship in 1993. Wapella minor hockey teams under the same name won provincial titles at the Under-15 D level in 1986 and at the Under-13 C level in 2017.

From Wapella to the NHL

Dave Dunn was born in Moosomin and raised in Wapella. At seventeen, in the 1965–66 season, he played defence for the Wapella Hawks in the Saskatchewan Intermediate Hockey Association. He went on to the University of Saskatchewan, then to the National Hockey League with the Vancouver Canucks and the Toronto Maple Leafs. In 1978 he won the Avco Cup, the championship of the World Hockey Association, with the Winnipeg Jets.

Chapter 10

Wapella Today

The prairie of the 1880s is gone, and so are many of the farms, schools and families that filled it. But Wapella is still here, and the story is still being written.

Oil and grain

Mixed farming has always been the backbone of the district: grain, livestock, and today some specialty crops. In 1952 oil was discovered in the region, and over the following decades it brought jobs and a measure of stability that farming alone could not.

Farming itself changed beyond recognition. In 1969 Barney Kaplun, the last Jewish farmer of the old colony, worked about a thousand acres, 650 of them under cultivation, growing wheat, oats, barley and flax and keeping about seventy-five cattle. His wheat averaged forty-five bushels an acre, barley fifty-five and oats eighty, far beyond anything the first settlers ever saw. He spent about a thousand dollars a year on fertilizer. His combine cost $15,000 and his equipment altogether about $70,000. Hired help was hard to find, because the new potash mines nearby paid better. Many neighbours had moved into town and drove out to their land, and some had sold their livestock to grow grain alone.

Schools and the town

The one-room country schools closed in the 1950s, and the district’s children rode buses into town. The population, which had approached five hundred in 1911, settled over the years into the low three hundreds; the 2021 census counted 278 people. The town keeps a small core of businesses, services and recreational facilities, several service clubs, and a museum housed in the former Anglican church.

Mingling Memories

In 1979, for the town’s seventy-fifth anniversary, the Wapella History Book Committee published Mingling Memories: A History of Wapella and Districts, a 259-page hardcover gathering township histories, pioneer family biographies and the story of the community’s growth. Like hundreds of community history books produced across the prairies in those years, it was written by volunteers and is treasured by the families in it. Copies are now hard to find. If you have one, it is worth keeping safe.

The elevator, again

In September 2017 the Wapella grain elevator was sold to Pipeline Foods of Minnesota, and its rail switch was reconnected to the CPR main line after more than fifteen years of silence. A hundred and thirty-five years after the first train, the railway was once again carrying Wapella’s grain to the world.
