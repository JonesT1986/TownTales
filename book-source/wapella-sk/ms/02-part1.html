Part One

The Land and the Line

Chapter 1

Before the Townsite

Long before there was a station, a grain elevator or a main street, the country around Wapella was home to Indigenous peoples who knew its rolling parkland, its sloughs and its stands of poplar in detail no surveyor would ever match.

The land where Wapella now stands lies in the southeast corner of what became Saskatchewan, about twenty miles west of the Manitoba border. It is aspen parkland: open prairie broken by bluffs of poplar and willow, low ridges, and countless small ponds that fill each spring with snowmelt. For centuries the Cree, the Saulteaux and the Nakoda (Assiniboine) travelled, hunted and lived across this country, and the Métis carried trade along its trails. The buffalo that once grazed here were the foundation of that world.

By the 1870s that world was changing quickly. The buffalo herds were collapsing, the Hudson’s Bay Company had surrendered its claim to Rupert’s Land, and the new Dominion of Canada was determined to open the West to settlement and build a railway across it. Before it could do either, the Crown sought treaties with the nations who lived there.

Treaty 4

In September 1874, at Fort Qu’Appelle, representatives of the Crown met leaders of the Cree, Saulteaux and Assiniboine peoples and concluded the Qu’Appelle Treaty, known today as Treaty 4. It covers a broad sweep of what is now southern Saskatchewan, with smaller parts of Alberta and Manitoba. Wapella stands within its boundaries.

For the Crown, the treaty opened the way for surveyors, the railway and homesteaders. For the First Nations who signed it, it carried promises of reserve land, annual payments, farming help, schools and the continued right to hunt and fish. How well those promises were kept is a story that is still being told, and it is part of the history of every town in the treaty area, Wapella included.

Near Wapella today, along the Trans-Canada Highway, a road sign marks the boundary area between Treaty 2 and Treaty 4. It greets travellers in Saulteaux, Lakota and Plains Cree, a small, daily reminder of whose land this was first.

What does “Wapella” mean?

Nobody knows for certain. The historian Cyril Leonoff, whose own family homesteaded here, wrote that the true meaning had been lost. The name is thought to come from a Sioux-related word used by the Assiniboine, and several meanings have been suggested over the years: “melting snow,” “gently falling snow,” “turning white,” “running water,” and “water underneath.”

The last of these has a practical ring to it. The district was known for good water close to the surface, and the Canadian Pacific Railway, which probably chose the name, drew water here for its steam engines for many years.

One thing the name is not: it has no connection to Chief Wapello of the Meskwaki, the namesake of the village of Wapella, Illinois.

Water, wood and good soil

The settlers who arrived in the 1880s judged land by three things: wood, water and soil. Wapella’s district scored well on all three. When a party of Jewish settlers went looking for land in 1888, they turned down country near Deloraine, Manitoba, because the soil was too light, and country near Regina because it was heavy gumbo clay. They chose the wooded land near Wapella because the poplar bluffs gave them logs for houses and fuel for the winter, and because water lay close to the surface.

That shallow water shows up again and again in the story: in the possible meaning of the town’s name, in the railway’s water stop, and in the wells that families dug by hand, sometimes sixty feet down before they struck a spring.

Chapter 2

Steel Comes West

In 1882 the Canadian Pacific Railway’s main line was pushing west across the prairie at a pace that astonished everyone who saw it. Where the track went, towns followed. Wapella was one of them.

The CPR’s transcontinental line reached Wapella in 1882 during its great westward drive. The townsite was laid out that same year. An early atlas listed Wapella as the second station inside the District of Assiniboia when travelling west, after Moosomin. In those days Saskatchewan did not yet exist as a province: this was the North-West Territories, and Assiniboia was one of its districts.

The railway decided almost everything about a prairie town in those years. It chose where the station would stand, which in turn decided where the main street would run, where the elevators would go and where the hotels would open their doors. It brought in settlers, lumber, machinery and mail, and it carried out wheat. The CPR also probably gave the town its name, and its locomotives drank from Wapella’s water for years afterwards.

A village by the tracks

Growth was slow at first. Around 1890 the village was still small: the CPR station, the Queen’s Hotel, one store, a blacksmith shop and a few houses. It does not sound like much, but each of those buildings mattered. The station connected the district to the world. The hotel housed travellers, land seekers and salesmen. The store sold everything from flour to nails, and the blacksmith kept the district’s horses shod and its ploughs sharp.

A post office is reported to have opened at Wapella on November 1, 1883. That date comes from a single online source and has not yet been checked against the federal post office records, so treat it as likely rather than proven. Either way, a post office in the early 1880s would have been the district’s link to family left behind in Scotland, England and Russia, and letters home were how many of the next settlers first heard of Wapella.

The first school

Settlers wanted schools almost as soon as they had roofs. Wapella School District No. 11 was one of the earliest in the Territories. The sources do not agree on the exact year: one account gives 1886, another counts Wapella among the first districts organized in 1885. Whichever is right, the town’s first school district dates from the middle of the 1880s, only a few years after the railway arrived. The district’s low number, eleven, tells the same story: Wapella was near the front of the line.

The branch line

In 1903 the CPR built a branch line through the district, connecting new settlements to the main line in the Wapella area. Towns such as Rocanville and Tantallon were founded after the branch went through. For Wapella, the branch confirmed its place as a junction for the farms around it.

The elevator

The grain elevator became the symbol of every prairie town, and Wapella’s still stands. Its story came full circle in September 2017, when it was sold to Pipeline Foods of Minnesota. With the sale, the elevator’s rail switch was reconnected to the CPR main line after sitting idle for more than fifteen years. The elevator handles several thousand tonnes of grain a year, and trains once again stop for Wapella’s wheat, more than 130 years after the first one came through.
