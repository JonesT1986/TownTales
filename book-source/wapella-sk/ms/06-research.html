Researching Your Wapella Family

Many readers will open this book looking for a grandparent or a great-grandparent. Here is where to look next. Most of these records are free, and many can be searched from home.

Homestead files

Almost every family who farmed in the district before the 1930s filed for a homestead. The Provincial Archives of Saskatchewan holds the homestead files for the province, and its online index can be searched by name and by land location. A homestead file can include the date a family arrived, where they came from, how many acres they broke each year, and the buildings they put up. If you know the quarter section, start there.

Newspapers

The Saskatchewan Historic Newspapers collection includes about 145 digitized issues of the Wapella Post from the Second World War years. Peel’s Prairie Provinces, at the University of Alberta, holds digitized runs of other early prairie newspapers, including papers from Moosomin and Brandon that reported regularly on Wapella. Search for a family name together with “Wapella.”

Census returns

The Canadian censuses of 1891, 1901, 1906, 1911, 1916, 1921, 1926 and 1931 are all open to the public and can be searched by name through Library and Archives Canada. In 1891 and 1901 Wapella was in the District of Assiniboia, North-West Territories; from 1906, in Saskatchewan.

Cemetery records

The Saskatchewan Genealogical Society’s cemetery index and the Saskatchewan Cemeteries Project transcriptions are the sources for the Roll of Remembrance in this book. Every name on the Roll also has its own page at towntales.ca, where you can add a photograph or a story.

The colony

For the Jewish farm colony, start with Cyril Leonoff’s article in the Manitoba Historical Society’s Transactions and the Dictionary of Canadian Biography entry on Abraham Klenman. The Jewish Heritage Centre of Western Canada in Winnipeg collects the records of prairie Jewish farm settlements.

Local books and people

Mingling Memories: A History of Wapella and Districts (1979) has family histories written by the families themselves. Ask at the Wapella museum, the town office and the regional library. And ask the oldest people you know. The best sources for the next edition of this book are not in any archive yet.
